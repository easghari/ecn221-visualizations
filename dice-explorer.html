Classical vs. Relative Frequency Probability: Rolling Two Dice

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1Two ways to assign a probability

Roll two fair dice. What is the probability that the two numbers add up to 7? There are two ways to answer.

  • Classical probability. List every possible outcome. If all of them are equally likely, the probability of an event is the share of outcomes in which it happens:

    P(E) = (number of outcomes in E) / (total number of outcomes)

    Two dice have 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely outcomes (the multiplication rule of counting), and 6 of them add up to 7, so P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6. No dice need to be rolled.
  • Relative frequency. Repeat the experiment many times and see how often the event happens:

    P(E) ≈ (number of times E happened) / (number of trials)

    This is how most business probabilities are found, because the outcomes are rarely equally likely: the share of customers who return a product, the share of loans that default, the share of flights that leave late.

How the two connect: with only a few trials, the relative frequency can be far from the true probability. As the number of trials grows, it settles down closer and closer to it. This is the law of large numbers, and it is why the relative frequency from a large amount of data is a reliable estimate of a probability.

4Reading the results

The classical probability comes from counting, before any dice are rolled; it is exact, but it only works because all 36 outcomes are equally likely. The relative frequency comes from data. Early on it swings widely: after 10 rolls it can easily be 0 or 0.4 for an event whose probability is 1/6. As the rolls pile up, the swings shrink and the line settles onto the dashed line. With 100 rolls it is typically within about 0.07 of the classical value; with 10,000 rolls, within about 0.007. Ten times the accuracy costs a hundred times the data.

The counts in the grid tell the same story outcome by outcome: each of the 36 cells comes up about 1/36 of the time in the long run, which is exactly the "equally likely" assumption that the classical definition relies on.

5Explore further

  1. Choose "The sum is 7". Count the gold cells and write the classical probability. Then roll slowly for 20 rolls and stop. How close is the relative frequency? Clear and repeat: do you get the same answer?
  2. Choose "The sum equals k" and try k = 2, 7 and 12. Why is 7 the most likely sum, even though each die is fair? Count the cells.
  3. Roll 100 times several times and write down the relative frequency each time. Then do the same with 10,000 rolls. How much do the results vary in each case?
  4. Choose "At least one die shows a 6". Count the gold cells. Now count the cells that are not gold: what event do they make up? Use the complement rule to check: P(at least one 6) = 1 − P(no 6) = 1 − 25/36.
  5. A student argues: "There are 11 possible sums, 2 through 12, so P(sum = 7) = 1/11." Roll 10,000 times. Which answer does the relative frequency support, 1/11 or 1/6? What is wrong with the student's reasoning?
  6. Think of a business probability, such as the chance that a customer returns a purchase. Could you find it with the classical definition? Why does it have to come from relative frequency?

Key point: the classical definition counts equally likely outcomes; the relative frequency definition counts what actually happens over many trials. When the outcomes really are equally likely, the two agree in the long run, and the law of large numbers says the relative frequency gets closer to the true probability as the number of trials grows.
