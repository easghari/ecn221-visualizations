1The idea behind the Central Limit Theorem

Suppose we take a random sample of n observations from some population and compute the sample mean x̄. If we took a different random sample, we would get a slightly different x̄. So the sample mean is itself a random variable, and it has its own distribution, called the sampling distribution of the sample mean.

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) tells us three things about that distribution, for any population with mean μ and standard deviation σ, as long as the sample size n is large enough:

  1. Center: the sample means are centered at the population mean, E(x̄) = μ.
  2. Spread: their standard deviation, called the standard error, is SE = σ / √n. Larger samples give less variable sample means.
  3. Shape: the distribution of x̄ is approximately normal, even when the population is not.

That last point is the surprising one, and it is why the normal distribution shows up everywhere in statistics. This page lets you check it by actually running the experiment.

2How the simulation works

Rather than taking one sample, we will let the computer take many samples and watch where the sample means land. The text below updates as you change the settings in step 3.

  1. One observation.
  2. One sample. That gives us one value of x̄, and that is one round of sampling.
  3. Repeat. Each round gives a new x̄.
  4. Plot. We put all of those x̄ values in a histogram. That histogram is the sampling distribution of the sample mean. We then compare it with the normal curve the CLT predicts, which has mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n.

The question we are testing: does the histogram of sample means look normal, centered at μ, with spread σ/√n, even if the population we started from looks nothing like a normal curve?

Notice the √n in the standard error. Going from n = 50 to n = 100 doubles the sample size but only shrinks the standard error by a factor of √2 ≈ 1.41, about 30%. To cut the standard error in half, you need four times as many observations.

5Explore further

  1. With the coin and n = 50, run the animation. Where do the sample means pile up? Then check: is the observed standard error close to 0.5/√50 ≈ 0.071?
  2. Set n = 5 flips and run it again. How many distinct values can the proportion of heads take? Does the normal curve still fit well? What does this say about "large enough" n?
  3. Set n = 100. Compare the predicted standard error with the n = 50 case. Why is it not half as large?
  4. Switch to the right-skewed population (think customer wait times) with n = 5, then n = 30. Does the sampling distribution inherit the skew? At what n does it look normal?
  5. Try the bimodal population. The population has two humps and almost nothing in the middle, yet where do the sample means land?
  6. Change the number of samples from 1,000 to 100. The shape gets rougher, but does the predicted standard error change? Why not?

Key point: the CLT describes the distribution of the sample mean across many samples, not the population and not the data in any one sample. The top chart keeps its shape no matter what; the bottom chart becomes normal as n grows. That is what lets us use normal-based confidence intervals and hypothesis tests for means even when the underlying data are not normal.
