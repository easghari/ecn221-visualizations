Confidence Intervals

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1What a confidence interval is, and what a 95% confidence level means

We want to know a population mean μ, but we only get to see one sample of n observations. The sample mean x̄ is our best single guess, but it will not equal μ exactly. A confidence interval turns that single guess into a range:

x̄ ± (critical value) × σ/√n    i.e.    x̄ ± margin of error

For a 95% interval with σ known, the critical value is z = 1.96; for 90% it is 1.645 and for 99% it is 2.576. When σ is unknown we use the sample standard deviation s and a t critical value instead.

Here is the part students most often get wrong. The population mean μ is a fixed number; it does not move. The interval is what is random, because it is built from a random sample. Take a different sample and you get a different x̄ and a different interval. Some of those intervals will contain μ; some will miss it.

The correct interpretation of a 95% confidence level: if we repeated the sampling process many times and built an interval from each sample, about 95% of those intervals would contain μ and about 5% would miss it. The 95% describes the long-run success rate of the method, not the chance that one particular interval is right. Any single interval either contains μ or it does not; we just do not know which.

That is a statement about repeating the process many times, which is exactly what a computer can do. This page lets you watch it happen.

When can you trust the stated confidence level? The critical values 1.96 (z) and tn−1 come from the normal and t distributions, so the promised 95% holds only if the sample mean x̄ is normally distributed. That is true in two situations:

  1. The population itself is normal. Then x̄ is exactly normal for any sample size.
  2. The sample is large. Then the Central Limit Theorem makes x̄ approximately normal whatever the population looks like. The usual rule of thumb is n ≥ 30; the more skewed the population, the larger n needs to be.

If neither holds, a non-normal population and a small sample, the interval is still computed the same way, but its real success rate can fall well short of 95%. The "heavily right-skewed" population in step 3 lets you see this happen, and then watch the success rate recover as n grows.

2How the simulation works

We play the role of someone who knows the truth. We pick a population, so we know μ exactly, then pretend we do not and let the computer sample from it over and over. The text below updates as you change the settings in step 3.

  1. One observation.
  2. One sample and one interval. That is what a researcher who only had this one sample would report.
  3. Check it. Because we know the true μ, we can check whether the interval actually contains it. Intervals that contain μ are drawn in maroon; intervals that miss are drawn in gold.
  4. Repeat and count. We keep a running count of how many of the intervals contained μ, and compare that percentage with the confidence level we chose.

The question we are testing: if we build confidence intervals over and over, does roughly of them really contain μ?

4Reading the results

The confidence level is a promise about the long run. The simulation checks whether the promise holds.

What the theory predicts

Population mean μ– Population SD σ– Sample size n– Confidence level– Critical value– Margin of error (σ known)– Is x̄ normally distributed?– Expected share of intervals containing μ–

What the simulation produced

Intervals built0 Intervals containing μ– Intervals missing μ–   … entirely below μ (too low)–   … entirely above μ (too high)– Observed share containing μ– Average interval width–

The observed share is the simulation's version of the confidence level. When x̄ is normal, the misses split evenly: at 95%, about 2.5% too low and 2.5% too high.

Two things trade off here. A higher confidence level means a larger critical value, so a wider interval: you catch μ more often, but you say less about where it is. A larger sample shrinks σ/√n, so the interval gets narrower while keeping the same success rate. Confidence level controls how often you are right; sample size controls how precise you are.

All of this rests on x̄ being normal. With a heavily skewed population and a small sample, the sampling distribution of x̄ is itself skewed, and two things go wrong. With the t interval, the sample standard deviation s tends to be small exactly when x̄ is small, so the intervals that should reach up to μ are too short: the success rate drops well below the stated level, with almost all the misses on one side. With the z interval (σ known), the total can still look close to 95%, but the misses are lopsided, which still means the stated level is not describing the method correctly. As n grows, the Central Limit Theorem straightens x̄ out and both problems fade.

5Explore further

  1. Run the default (uniform population, n = 30, 95%, 100 intervals). Count the gold intervals. Is it close to 5? Run it again: the count changes, but what does it hover around?
  2. Switch to 1,000 intervals and draw all at once. The observed share should now be very close to 95%. Why does more repetition make the percentage more stable, even though each individual interval is just as uncertain as before?
  3. Change the confidence level to 80%, then 99%. Watch two things: the width of the intervals and the number of gold ones. Explain the trade-off in one sentence.
  4. Keep 95% and move n from 30 to 120. The intervals get narrower, but does the share containing μ change? What does that tell you about what the confidence level does and does not control?
  5. Pick one gold (missed) interval and look at where its x̄ is. Could the researcher who drew that sample have known their interval was wrong?
  6. When the assumptions fail. Choose the heavily right-skewed population, the t interval (σ unknown), 95%, and n = 10. Set the number of intervals to 1,000 and draw them all. The population is not normal and the sample is small, so x̄ is not normal. What share of the intervals actually contains μ? (Expect something in the mid-80s, not 95.) Look at the miss split: which side are almost all the misses on?
  7. The Central Limit Theorem to the rescue. Keep everything the same and raise n to 30, then 100, then 200, then 500, drawing 1,000 intervals each time. Write down the observed share at each size. How quickly does it climb back toward 95%? Is n = 30 enough for this population, and what does that tell you about the "n ≥ 30" rule of thumb?
  8. Repeat the last two prompts with the z interval (σ known). The total share may look close to 95% even at n = 10. Now compare the "too low" and "too high" counts: are they close to 2.5% each? Why is a 95% interval whose misses all fall on one side still not doing what it claims?
  9. Compare with the normal population at n = 10 and the t interval. Why does it deliver 95% even with a tiny sample?

Key point: a 95% confidence level is a property of the procedure, verified by repeating it. It does not mean "there is a 95% chance μ is in my interval." In practice you get one sample and one interval, and you never find out whether it was one of the maroon ones or one of the gold ones. The confidence level tells you how good the method is in the long run, which is the best anyone can say from a single sample.
