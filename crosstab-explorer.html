Crosstabulation: Summarizing Two Variables at Once

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1One table, three kinds of percentages

A crosstabulation (two-way table) summarizes two categorical variables together. Here, a retailer records, for each sale in a month, the store location (the rows) and the payment method (the columns). Each cell counts the sales with that combination; the row totals and column totals at the edges summarize each variable on its own.

The counts can be turned into percentages in three ways, and each answers a different question:

  • Row percentages divide each cell by its row total. Each row adds up to 100%. They answer: "Within each location, how do customers pay?"
  • Column percentages divide each cell by its column total. Each column adds up to 100%. They answer: "Among customers who pay a certain way, where do they shop?"
  • Percent of the total divides each cell by the grand total. All the cells together add up to 100%. It answers: "What share of all sales has this combination?"

Is there a relationship between the two variables? Compare the row percentages across the rows. If every location has the same mix of payment methods, location and payment method are unrelated. If the mix changes from one location to another, the two variables are related.

4Reading the results

The same cell can produce three very different percentages, because each one divides by a different group. In January of the first scenario, 30 of the 150 Downtown sales were paid in cash: that is 20% of Downtown sales (row percentage), but 37.5% of all cash sales (column percentage), and 7.5% of all 400 sales (percent of the total). None of these is "the" answer; the question decides which one you need.

The row-percentage bars make relationships easy to spot. In the first scenario, the bars for the three locations have different mixes, month after month: Airport customers rarely pay cash and Mall customers use mobile wallets less. In the second scenario, the overall mix changes from month to month, but within any one month every location's bar has exactly the same mix (in January: 20% cash, 55% card and 25% mobile), so knowing the location tells you nothing about how a customer pays.

5Explore further

  1. In January of the first scenario, click the Downtown / Cash cell. Read its four descriptions. Which percentage answers "What share of Downtown customers pay cash?" Which answers "What share of cash customers shop Downtown?"
  2. Switch to row percentages. Which rows add up to 100%? Now switch to column percentages. What adds up to 100% now?
  3. Using the row percentages, at which location are customers most likely to pay with a mobile wallet? Why would the counts alone be misleading for this question?
  4. Switch to the second scenario and look at the row-percentage chart. What do you notice about the three bars? What does that say about the relationship between location and payment method?
  5. Click "Show a different month" several times in each scenario, watching the row-percentage chart. In the first scenario, which location has the smallest share of cash payments each month? In the second scenario, do the three bars ever differ from one another within a month?
  6. In January of the first scenario, a manager says: "Half of our cash sales happen at the Mall, so Mall customers prefer cash." Use the row and column percentages to decide whether the conclusion follows.

Key point: a crosstabulation can be turned into percentages by row, by column, or of the total, and each answers a different question. To check whether two categorical variables are related, compare the row (or column) percentages: if the mix is the same in every row, there is no relationship.
