Hypothesis Tests: Interpreting α and Power

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1The logic of a hypothesis test, and what α really means

A hypothesis test starts with a claim about the population mean, the null hypothesis H₀: μ = μ₀, and asks whether the data are consistent with it. We take a sample, compute x̄, and measure how far it is from μ₀ in standard-error units:

test statistic   z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)    or    t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)

If H₀ is true, this statistic follows a known distribution (standard normal for z, a t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom for t). The p-value is the probability of getting a statistic at least this extreme if H₀ were true. If the p-value is below the significance level α, we reject H₀; otherwise we fail to reject it. Equivalently, we reject when the statistic falls in the rejection region beyond the critical value.

The test can be wrong in two ways:

H₀ is actually trueH₀ is actually false
We reject H₀Type I error (false alarm)
happens with probability α		Correct decision
probability = power
We fail to reject H₀Correct decision
probability = 1 − α		Type II error (missed effect)
probability = β = 1 − power

What α = 0.05 means: if H₀ is true and we ran the test over and over on fresh samples, we would wrongly reject it about 5% of the time. We choose α; it is the false-alarm rate we are willing to live with. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, and a p-value is not that probability either.

Like the confidence level on the previous page, α is a promise about the long run. This page checks the promise by running the test thousands of times, first when H₀ is true and then when it is false.

Power depends on three things you control in step 3: how far the true mean is from μ₀ (a bigger gap is easier to detect), the sample size (a larger n shrinks the standard error, so the same gap is more standard errors wide), and α (a larger α makes rejecting easier, which raises power but also raises the false-alarm rate). You cannot lower both error rates at once except by collecting more data.

5Explore further

  1. Run the default (true μ = μ₀ = 5, n = 30, α = 0.05, 1,000 tests). H₀ is true, so every rejection is a Type I error. Is the rejection rate near 5%? Set α to 0.01 and rerun. What changes?
  2. Watch the first few animated tests. Find one that rejects even though H₀ is true. What did its sample mean look like, and could the person running that test have known they were making a mistake?
  3. Move the true mean to 5.5 with n = 30. Now H₀ is false, so each rejection is correct and the rejection rate is the power. Roughly what is it? Then raise n to 120. Why did the power jump?
  4. Keep μ = 5.5 and compare α = 0.01 with α = 0.10. Which gives more power, and what is the price you pay for it?
  5. Set μ = 5.5 and switch the alternative from two-tailed to right-tailed. Power goes up. Now set μ = 4.5 and keep it right-tailed. What happens, and why is choosing the tail after seeing the data cheating?
  6. Set μ back to 5 (H₀ true), choose the right-skewed population, the t test, and n = 5. Does the Type I error rate stay at α? Try n = 40. This is why the t test needs larger samples when the population is skewed.

Key point: a p-value tells you how surprising your data would be if H₀ were true; it does not tell you the probability that H₀ is true. "Fail to reject" is not the same as "H₀ is true", and when the true mean is close to μ₀ or the sample is small, a test can miss a real difference most of the time. α controls false alarms; sample size is what buys you power.
