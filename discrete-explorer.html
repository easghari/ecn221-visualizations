Discrete Random Variables: Binomial and Poisson Distributions

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1Random variables you can count

A discrete random variable takes values you can list and count: the number of cars a dealership sells today, the number of customers who respond to an email, the number of calls a help desk receives in an hour. Its probability distribution lists every possible value x and its probability P(x). Every probability is between 0 and 1, and together they add up to 1.

Two numbers summarize a distribution:

Expected value (mean):   μ = E(X) = Σ x · P(x)

Variance:   σ² = Σ (x − μ)² · P(x),    standard deviation σ = √σ²

The expected value is the long-run average: if you observed the variable over and over, the average of the values would settle on μ. It does not have to be a value the variable can actually take; a dealership can sell 2.2 cars per day on average but never 2.2 cars on a given day.

Two named distributions come up again and again in business:

  • Binomial. Count the successes in n independent trials, each with the same probability of success p (each of n customers buys or does not; each of n items is defective or not). P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, where C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!) counts the ways to choose which k trials are the successes. Mean μ = np, standard deviation σ = √(np(1 − p)). See panel A.
  • Poisson. Count how many times an event happens in a fixed interval of time or space, when events happen independently at an average rate λ per interval (calls per hour, customers per hour, typos per page). P(X = k) = e−λ λᵏ / k!. Its mean and its variance are both equal to λ. See panel B.

2How the two panels work

  1. Panel A: the binomial distribution on a Galton board. Each ball is one group of n customers. At each row of pegs, one customer decides: the ball bounces right if that customer buys (probability p) and left if not. The bin where a ball lands is the number of customers who bought. Thousands of balls pile up into the binomial distribution. A calculator below finds P(X = k), P(X ≤ k) or P(X ≥ k) from the formula.
  2. Panel B: Poisson arrivals. Customers arrive at random, at an average rate of λ per hour. The timeline shows the arrivals in one interval; the count for that interval is added to the histogram, which builds up against the Poisson distribution.

4Reading the results

Panel A. Each ball makes n independent left/right decisions with the same probability p, exactly the binomial setup. There are C(n, k) different paths into bin k, and each has probability pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, which is where the formula comes from. With p = 0.5 the pile is symmetric around n/2; with a small p it bunches up on the left and trails off to the right.

Panel B. Arrivals come in clumps and gaps even though the rate is constant; that randomness is what the Poisson distribution describes. The average count settles on λ and, unusually, so does the variance. Doubling the length of the interval doubles λ, so it shifts and widens the whole distribution.

5Explore further

  1. Panel A: with n = 10, compare p = 0.5, p = 0.1 and p = 0.9. Describe the shape of the pile in each case. Where is np on each one?
  2. Panel A: set n = 10 and p = 0.2. Use the calculator to find P(X ≥ 3), the chance that at least 3 of 10 customers buy. Then drop 2,000 balls and check what share of them landed in bins 3 and above.
  3. Panel A: keep p = 0.2 and compare n = 5 with n = 20. How does the shape change as n grows?
  4. Panel B: with 4 customers per hour and 1-hour intervals, simulate 1,000 hours. Are the average count and the variance of the counts both close to 4? Now switch to 2-hour intervals. What happens to λ, and to the shape?
  5. Panel B: compare 1 customer per hour with 10 per hour. When is the Poisson distribution strongly skewed, and when does it look roughly symmetric?

Key point: a discrete probability distribution is a list of values and their probabilities, and its expected value is the average you would see in the long run, not the value you should expect on any one occasion. The binomial distribution counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials; the Poisson distribution counts events in a fixed interval when they occur at a steady average rate.
