Probability Relationships: Complements, Unions, Conditional Probability and Independence

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1How probabilities of related events fit together

A store tracks 100 customers. Event A: the customer saw the store's online ad. Event B: the customer made a purchase. Each customer either is or is not in A, and either is or is not in B, so every customer falls into exactly one of four groups: both, A only, B only, or neither. All the probability relationships come from counting these groups.

  • Complement. "Not A" is everything outside A: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Intersection. "A and B" means both happen: the customers who saw the ad and bought. Its probability, P(A and B), is a joint probability.
  • Union and the addition rule. "A or B" means at least one happens. Adding P(A) and P(B) counts the customers in both events twice, so the overlap is subtracted once: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).
  • Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together: P(A and B) = 0. Only then does the addition rule simplify to P(A) + P(B).
  • Conditional probability. "A given B" restricts attention to the customers in B and asks what share of them are in A: P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). Rearranged, this is the multiplication rule: P(A and B) = P(B) · P(A | B).
  • Independent events. Knowing that one happened does not change the probability of the other: P(B | A) = P(B), or equivalently P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B).

Two common mix-ups: P(A | B) is not the same as P(B | A) (the share of buyers who saw the ad is not the share of ad-viewers who bought), and mutually exclusive is not the same as independent. In fact, two mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent: if one happens, you know for certain the other did not.

4Reading the results

Every probability on this page is a count of customers divided by a count of customers. For P(A), P(A and B) and P(A or B), the denominator is all 100 customers. For a conditional probability, the denominator shrinks: P(B | A) only looks at the customers in A, which is why the other dots fade out. That shrinking of the "sample space" is the whole idea of conditioning.

With the ad scenario, 15 of the 40 ad viewers bought (37.5%), while only 25% of all customers bought: seeing the ad and buying are dependent, and the ad appears to be associated with more purchases. In the "no effect" scenario, 10 of the 40 ad viewers bought, exactly the overall 25%, so the events are independent. In the cash/card scenario, no customer is in both groups: the events are mutually exclusive, and therefore dependent, because knowing a customer paid with cash tells you for certain they did not pay by card.

5Explore further

  1. With the ad scenario, choose P(A or B). Count the gold dots. Why is P(A) + P(B) = 0.65 the wrong answer? Which customers would be counted twice?
  2. Choose P(B | A) and then P(B). Does seeing the ad change the chance that a customer buys? By how much?
  3. Compare P(A | B) with P(B | A). Describe each one in a sentence about customers. Why are they different numbers?
  4. Switch to "the ad has no effect". Compare P(B | A) with P(B), and P(A and B) with P(A) · P(B). What does independence mean in words here?
  5. Switch to "paid with cash / paid by card". What is P(A and B)? Does the addition rule still need the subtraction? Are the two events independent?
  6. Keep P(A) = 0.40 and P(B) = 0.25 and slide P(A and B) from its smallest to its largest value. At which value are A and B independent? What do the dots look like at the two extremes?
  7. Choose P(not A). Check the complement rule by counting the dots that are not in A.

Key point: complements, unions, intersections and conditional probabilities are all ways of counting the same four groups of customers. The addition rule subtracts the overlap so it is not counted twice; a conditional probability changes the denominator to the group you know about; and independence means that knowing one event happened leaves the probability of the other unchanged.
