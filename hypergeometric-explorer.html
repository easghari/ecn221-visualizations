Binomial vs. Hypergeometric: Sampling With and Without Replacement

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1Two ways to count defective parts in a sample

A shipment contains N parts, and K of them are defective. In the language of these distributions, finding a defective part is a success, and the probability of success on a draw, finding a defective item, is p = K/N. An inspector takes a sample of n parts and counts X, the number of defective parts in the sample. Which distribution describes X depends on one detail: whether each inspected part goes back into the shipment before the next one is drawn.

  • With replacement: binomial. Every draw is made from the full shipment, so every draw has the same probability of success, p = K/N, and the draws are independent. P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ.
  • Without replacement: hypergeometric. Each inspected part is set aside, so the shipment shrinks and its mix changes after every draw. The draws are not independent: finding a defective part makes the next one a little less likely to be defective. P(X = k) = C(K, k) · C(N − K, n − k) / C(N, n): the number of ways to pick k of the K defective parts and n − k of the good ones, out of all the ways to pick n parts.

Both distributions have the same mean, μ = n · K/N. They differ in spread:

Binomial:   σ = √( n p (1 − p) )

Hypergeometric:   σ = √( n p (1 − p) ) · √( (N − n) / (N − 1) )

The extra factor, the finite population correction, is less than 1, so sampling without replacement gives a less variable count. Sampling without replacement can never inspect the same part twice, so each draw adds new information about the shipment.

When can you use the binomial instead? When the sample is a small fraction of the population, setting parts aside barely changes the shipment, and the two distributions are almost identical. The usual rule of thumb is n/N ≤ 5%. Real inspections are almost always done without replacement; the binomial is the convenient approximation that works when the shipment is large.

4Reading the results

Panel A. On the left, the chance that the next part is defective never changes, and the same part can be inspected more than once (its badge then shows 2 or more). That is the binomial setup: independent draws with a constant p. On the right, every defective part found lowers the chance for the next draw, and every good part found raises it. The draws depend on each other, which is why a different distribution, the hypergeometric, is needed.

Panel B. The two sets of bars always balance at the same mean, but with a small shipment the hypergeometric bars are bunched more tightly around it. As the shipment grows with the share defective held fixed, setting parts aside makes less and less difference: the correction factor approaches 1 and the bars merge. The acceptance probability shows why this matters in practice: for a small shipment, using the binomial gives the wrong chance of accepting the shipment.

  1. Panel A, with N = 20, K = 5 and n = 8: inspect the parts one at a time. On the right, what happens to the chance of a defective part each time a defective one is found? Each time a good one is found?
  2. Panel A: inspect all 8 parts a few times. Does the left inspector ever inspect the same part twice? Why can that never happen on the right?
  3. Panel A: set n = N, so the inspector on the right inspects the whole shipment. How many defective parts must the right inspector find? Is the same true on the left?
  4. Panel B, with N = 20, 25% defective and n = 8: compare the two sets of bars. Which one is bunched more tightly around the middle? How does that match the finite population correction in step 1?
  5. Panel B: keep 25% defective and n = 8, and slide N up to 1,000. At what shipment size do the two sets of bars become hard to tell apart? What is n/N there?
  6. Panel B: with N = 20, n = 8 and c = 1, compare P(accept) under the two models. If the inspector used the binomial for a shipment this small, would the acceptance rule look stricter or more lenient than it really is?

Key point: the binomial distribution assumes every draw has the same chance of success, independently of the others. Sampling without replacement from a finite population breaks that assumption, and the hypergeometric distribution is the exact answer. When the sample is a small fraction of the population (about 5% or less), the difference is negligible and the binomial is a fine approximation.
