Correlation: The Strength of a Linear Relationship

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1What covariance and correlation measure

Covariance and correlation describe how two variables, x and y, move together. Both start from the same idea: for each observation, multiply how far x is from its mean by how far y is from its mean.

Population covariance:   σxy = Σ (xi − μx)(yi − μy) / N

Sample covariance:   sxy = Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / (n − 1)

When large values of x tend to go with large values of y, most products are positive and the covariance is positive. Its size, though, depends on the units: measure y in cents instead of dollars and the covariance is 100 times bigger. The correlation coefficient removes the units by dividing by both standard deviations:

ρ = σxy / (σx σy)   (population),    r = sxy / (sx sy)   (sample)

What the correlation tells you, and what it does not:

  • It is always between −1 and +1, and it has no units.
  • Its sign gives the direction: positive when y tends to rise with x, negative when it tends to fall.
  • Its size measures how tightly the points cluster around a straight line: ±1 means they lie exactly on one. It does not measure how steep the line is.
  • A correlation near 0 means no linear relationship. The variables can still be strongly related in a curved way.
  • A single unusual point can change the correlation a lot.

4Reading the results

Panel A. The slope tells you how much y changes, on average, when x goes up by one unit. The correlation tells you how reliably the points follow that line. A steep line with points scattered far from it has a large slope and a weak correlation; a gentle line with points hugging it has a small slope and a strong correlation. The only thing the slope and the correlation always share is their sign.

Panel B. In the U-shaped pattern, y is completely determined by x, yet the correlation is about 0: the points on the left pull the covariance one way and the points on the right pull it the other way. The circle does the same. A single outlier can drag a correlation of 0.99 down sharply, and two separate groups can create a high correlation even when there is no relationship inside either group. Notice that the population and sample columns always give the same correlation, because the N or n − 1 cancels out of the ratio.

5Explore further

  1. Panel A, starting setup: which plot has the steeper line? Which has the stronger correlation? Explain in one sentence why these are different questions.
  2. Panel A, Plot 1: keep the slope at 3 and slide the scatter down to 0. What happens to r? Now keep the scatter at 0 and change the slope to 0.5, then to −2. What happens to r?
  3. Panel A: make both plots have r close to 0.9, one with slope 0.5 and one with slope 3. What did you have to change?
  4. Panel B, "U-shaped curve": is there a relationship between x and y? What is r? What does r = 0 mean, then?
  5. Panel B, "Straight line plus one outlier": drag the outlier onto the line and watch r. Then drag a point from the middle of the line far away. Which single point moves r the most?
  6. Panel B, "Two separate groups": the correlation is high. Is there a relationship inside either group? What does this warn you about when you combine data from two different groups?

Key point: the correlation coefficient measures the direction and strength of a linear relationship: how tightly the points follow a straight line. It is not the slope, it can miss a strong curved relationship, and a single unusual point can change it. Always look at the scatter plot before trusting the number.
