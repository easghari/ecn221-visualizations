Correlation vs. Causation: Simpson's Paradox

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1When the data seem to say the opposite of the truth

A call center manager looks at last quarter's data. Each element of the study is one shift. For each shift there are three variables: the average number of service representatives on duty, the average time a customer waited, and how busy the shift was. Each shift's three values make up one observation.

The manager plots wait time against staffing and sees that shifts with more representatives had longer waits. Should the call center cut staff to shorten waits?

  • Correlation is not causation. Two variables can move together without one causing the other. A third variable can drive both.
  • A lurking (confounding) variable is that third variable. Here it is how busy the shift is: the manager schedules more representatives for busy shifts, and busy shifts have long waits however many people are working.
  • Simpson's paradox is the most dramatic version: a relationship that holds within every group reverses direction when the groups are combined. Within each level of busyness, more representatives means shorter waits; across all shifts together, it looks like the opposite.

4Reading the results

With all shifts together, the trend line slopes up: more representatives, longer waits. Separate the shifts by how busy they were and every group's line slopes down: within quiet shifts, within normal shifts and within peak shifts, more representatives mean shorter waits. The overall upward trend is created by the groups sitting in different places: peak shifts have both the most staff and the longest waits, quiet shifts have both the least staff and the shortest waits.

The sliders show where the paradox comes from. When busy shifts are not given extra staff (the first slider at 0), the groups line up on top of each other and the overall trend agrees with the trend inside each group. When the true effect of staffing is set to 0, the groups are flat, yet the overall correlation is still positive: a correlation produced entirely by the lurking variable.

5Explore further

  1. Start with "All shifts together". What would the manager conclude about adding staff? Now switch to "Separate by how busy the shift was". What should the manager conclude instead?
  2. Identify the elements, the variables and one observation in this data set. Which variables are quantitative and which is categorical?
  3. Slide "extra staff scheduled for busy shifts" slowly from strong to 0, watching the overall trend line. At what point does it change direction?
  4. Set the true effect of one more representative to 0. Is there still a correlation between staffing and wait time when all shifts are combined? Does that correlation mean staffing causes longer waits?
  5. Think of another example from business or the news where a lurking variable could create a misleading correlation, such as ice cream sales and drowning incidents, or hospital stays and patient outcomes. What is the lurking variable?

Key point: a correlation does not, by itself, show that one variable causes the other. A lurking variable can create a correlation, hide one, or even reverse it, as in Simpson's paradox. Before concluding that one variable causes another, look for lurking variables and compare like with like.
