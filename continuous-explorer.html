Continuous Distributions: Probability as Area

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1How probability works when a variable can take any value

A discrete random variable, like the number of heads in 10 flips, has a list of possible values, and each one has a probability. A continuous random variable, like a delivery time or a stock return, can take any value in a range. There are infinitely many of them, so no single value can carry any probability: for a continuous X, P(X = a) = 0 for every a. Probability lives in intervals, and it is measured as area:

A continuous distribution is described by a probability density function f(x), a curve with total area 1 under it. The probability that X lands between a and b is the area under the curve between them:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under f(x) from a to b = F(b) − F(a)

where F(x) = P(X ≤ x) is the cumulative distribution function, the area to the left of x. Because single points have zero area, ≤ and < give the same answer: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(a < X < b).

Two continuous distributions carry this chapter:

  • Uniform on [α, β]. Every value in the range is equally likely, so the density is flat: f(x) = 1 / (β − α) for α ≤ x ≤ β and 0 elsewhere. Areas are rectangles: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = (b − a) / (β − α). Mean (α + β)/2, standard deviation (β − α)/√12.
  • Normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ. The bell curve, symmetric about μ, with σ setting how wide it is. There is no simple formula for its areas, so we convert to the standard normal Z ~ N(0, 1) using the z-score z = (x − μ) / σ, which says how many standard deviations x is from the mean, and look up or compute P(X ≤ x) = P(Z ≤ z) = Φ(z). One table serves every normal distribution.

The empirical rule for any normal distribution: about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three. It is just three areas under the bell curve, and you can check it by simulation in panel C.

2How the three panels work

  1. Panel A: probability as area. Choose a distribution and set its parameters. Then drag the gold handles on the axis (or use the sliders) to pick an interval. The area under the density between the handles fills in and the probability is printed, along with the calculation that produced it. The chart below it shows the same probability as a rise in the CDF. Switch to "find x for a given probability" to run the calculation backward, from a probability to a cutoff value.
  2. Panel B: every normal is the standard normal in disguise. Pick any μ and σ and a value x. The top chart shades the area to the left (or right) of x under N(μ, σ); the bottom chart shades the area to the left (or right) of z = (x − μ)/σ under N(0, 1). The two areas are always identical. That is why one z-table is enough.
  3. Panel C: the empirical rule, checked. Draw hundreds of values from a normal distribution, drop them under the curve, and count how many land within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean. Compare the counts with 68%, 95% and 99.7%.

4Reading the results

Panel A. For the uniform distribution the shaded area is a rectangle, so the probability is just its width divided by the total width, and the CDF is a straight ramp. For the normal distribution there is no formula for the area; the calculation box shows the real procedure: convert the endpoints to z-scores, look up Φ for each, subtract. Try pushing the two handles together: the area, and the probability, go to zero, which is what P(X = a) = 0 means.

Panel B. The z-score is a change of units. It measures distance from the mean in standard deviations, so N(100, 15) at x = 120 and N(0, 1) at z = 1.33 are the same point in the same distribution, described in two different rulers. Any question about any normal distribution becomes a question about the one standard normal.

Panel C. The 68 / 95 / 99.7 numbers are not a separate fact to memorize; they are the areas under the standard normal between ±1, ±2 and ±3. With 500 draws the observed shares wander a couple of percentage points from the exact areas; with 10,000 they settle onto them. That gap between observed and exact is the same sampling variability that the later chapters are built on.

5Explore further

  1. Panel A, uniform on [20, 80]: set a = 30 and b = 45. Then move both handles 10 units to the right. Does the probability change? Now try the same thing with the normal distribution. Why does it change there?
  2. Panel A, uniform: make the range very narrow, say [48, 52]. Read the height of the density off the axis. It is greater than 1. Why is that not a problem, if probabilities can never exceed 1?
  3. Panel A, normal μ = 50, σ = 10: find P(40 ≤ X ≤ 60), P(30 ≤ X ≤ 70) and P(20 ≤ X ≤ 80). You have just rediscovered the empirical rule.
  4. Panel A, "find x" mode with μ = 50, σ = 10: find the cutoff for the top 10% (p = 0.90), then the top 1%. How does the answer relate to the z-values 1.28 and 2.33?
  5. Panel B: set μ = 100, σ = 15, x = 130, then change σ to 30 while leaving x at 130. The shaded area grows, yet x did not move. Explain in terms of z.
  6. Panel C: draw 500 values and note the ±1σ share. Click Draw again several times. How much does the share wander? Switch to 10,000 and repeat.
  7. Panel C: change μ and σ to anything you like and redraw. Do the shares depend on μ or σ at all? What does that tell you about the empirical rule?

Key point: for a continuous random variable, probability is area under the density, so it belongs to intervals, never to single points. The normal distribution's areas all come from one curve, the standard normal, through the z-score. Everything later in the course that uses a z-table, a confidence interval, or a p-value is reading an area off that one curve.
