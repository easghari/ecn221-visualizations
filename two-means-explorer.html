Comparing Two Means: Is the Gap Real?

ECN 221 Business Statistics · Instructor: Ehsan Asghari-Ghara

1Testing whether two populations have the same mean

Do customers at store A spend more than customers at store B? Does the new training program raise productivity compared with the old one? Questions like these compare two population means, μ₁ and μ₂, using one independent sample from each. The null hypothesis says there is no difference:

H₀: μ₁ − μ₂ = 0     test statistic = (x̄₁ − x̄₂ − 0) / SE(x̄₁ − x̄₂)

The new ingredient is the standard error of the difference. Two sample means each have their own sampling variability, and when we subtract them the variabilities add:

SE(x̄₁ − x̄₂) = √( σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂ )

Which version of the formula we use depends on what we know about the population standard deviations:

CaseStandard errorStatistic
σ₁ and σ₂ known√(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)z, standard normal
σ unknown, assumed equal (pooled)sp·√(1/n₁ + 1/n₂), where sp² pools the two sample variancest, n₁ + n₂ − 2 df
σ unknown, not assumed equal (Welch)√(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)t, df from the Welch formula

The key intuition: even when two populations are identical, two samples from them will never have exactly the same mean. The question is never "are x̄₁ and x̄₂ different?" (they always are) but "is the gap between them larger than the gap we would expect from sampling variability alone?" The standard error of the difference is the yardstick for that.

The SD of the observed gaps is the simulation's version of the standard error of the difference.

Look at the two variance pieces, σ₁²/n₁ and σ₂²/n₂. The standard error is driven by the larger of the two. If one group is small or noisy, making the other group huge barely helps: the yardstick stays wide, and so the test stays weak. Balanced samples are the efficient way to spend a data-collection budget.

5Explore further

  1. Run the default: identical populations (μ₁ = μ₂ = 50, σ = 10, n = 30 each). Watch the first few tests. The two sample means are never equal, yet most tests correctly fail to reject. How wide is the band of "ordinary" gaps?
  2. Still with identical populations, is the rejection rate near α = 0.05? Every rejection here is a Type I error; find one in the animation and look at how big its gap was.
  3. Set μ₂ = 52 (a real 2-unit gap) with n = 30 each. The power will be low, around 12%. The test misses a true difference most of the time. Raise both n to 150. What is the power now?
  4. Set μ₂ = 52 and n₁ = 10, n₂ = 10. Then raise n₁ to 200 while leaving n₂ = 10. Compare the SE of the gap and the power. Why did adding 190 observations to group 1 help so little?
  5. Set σ₁ = 18 with n₁ = 10 and σ₂ = 4 with n₂ = 60, means equal (the noisy group is the small one). Run the pooled t test, then the Welch t test. The pooled test's false-alarm rate explodes to around 40%, while Welch stays near 5%. Why does pooling go so wrong when the small sample is the noisy one? Now swap the σ values: the pooled test becomes far too conservative instead.
  6. Switch the shape to right-skewed with n = 5 each and the pooled t test, means equal. Does the false-alarm rate stay near α? Try n = 40 each.

Key point: a difference between two sample means is not evidence of a difference between the populations until it is measured against the standard error of the difference. That standard error combines both groups' variability, which is why it is wider than either group's own standard error, and why the weaker group sets the limit on what the test can detect.
